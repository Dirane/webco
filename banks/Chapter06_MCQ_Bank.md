## SECTION B: ANSWER KEY WITH EXPLANATIONS

**1. B — Refract (bend) the light.**
In a light microscope, visible light passes through the specimen and then through glass lenses, which bend the light so that the image is magnified.

**2. C — Resolution.**
Magnification is the ratio of image size to real size; contrast is visible difference in brightness between parts of the sample; resolution is the measure of image clarity.

**3. C — About 1,000 times.**
Beyond this, the resolution of standard light microscopy is too low to study organelles.

**4. D — A ribosome.**
Ribosomes (about 12 nm) require an electron microscope or super-resolution light microscopy. Most plant and animal cells (10–100 μm), human egg cells (~100 μm), and frog eggs (~2 mm) are within light-microscope range.

**5. B — Phase-contrast microscopy.**
It amplifies density variations to enhance contrast in unstained cells. Most stains require cells to be preserved, which kills them.

**6. B — Labeling with fluorescent dyes or antibodies.**
These labels absorb ultraviolet radiation and emit visible light, revealing the location of specific molecules.

**7. B — A laser creates a single plane of fluorescence.**
Out-of-focus light from other planes is eliminated, and many sharp planes can be combined into a 3-D reconstruction. Option C describes deconvolution; option D describes super-resolution.

**8. B — Scanning electron microscopy (SEM).**
SEM focuses a beam of electrons onto the specimen surface, giving images that look three-dimensional.

**9. C — A transmission electron microscope.**
TEMs focus a beam of electrons **through** a thin section and are used mainly to study internal cell structure.

**10. B — Rapid freezing preserves a near-native state.**
Specimens are frozen below −160°C, locking molecules in a rigid state; no preservatives or crystallization are needed. Cryo-EM complements X-ray crystallography.

**11. C — Nuclei and cellular debris.**
The homogenate is first spun at 1,000 g for 10 minutes; the heaviest components pellet first.

**12. D — Last, at 150,000 g for 3 hours.**
The sequence is: nuclei (1,000 g) → mitochondria/chloroplasts (2,000 g) → microsomes (80,000 g) → ribosomes (150,000 g). Smaller components require greater force and longer time.

**13. B — Plasma membrane, cytosol, chromosomes, and ribosomes.**
These four features are common to all cells. Nuclei and membrane-bound organelles are eukaryotic features.

**14. B — Nucleoid.**
The nucleoid is the region where prokaryotic DNA is located; it is not enclosed by a membrane.

**15. C — No membrane-bound organelles and no true nucleus.**
Prokaryotic cytoplasm is bound only by the plasma membrane. Options A, B, and D describe eukaryotic cells.

**16. B — Volume increases proportionately more than surface area.**
This geometric constraint sets an upper limit on cell size, because the plasma membrane must service the entire cell volume with adequate exchange of oxygen, nutrients, and waste.

**17. C — Greater total surface area but the same total volume.**
The 5-unit cube has 150 square units of surface and 125 cubic units of volume (ratio 1.2). The 125 small cubes have 750 square units of surface and the same 125 cubic units of volume (ratio 6). Subdividing raises the surface-area-to-volume ratio without changing volume.

**18. B — A double membrane perforated by pore complexes.**
Each of the two membranes is itself a lipid bilayer; pore complexes regulate entry and exit of molecules.

**19. A — Lines the inner surface and maintains nuclear shape.**
It is composed of proteins. There is also evidence for a nuclear matrix, a framework of protein fibers throughout the nuclear interior.

**20. B — Chromatin.**
Each chromosome contains one DNA molecule associated with proteins; chromatin condenses into discrete chromosomes as the cell prepares to divide.

**21. B — Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) synthesis.**
The nucleolus lies within the nucleus and is the site of rRNA production.

**22. B — Ribosomal RNA and protein.**
A ribosome consists of a large and a small subunit.

**23. B — Outside of the ER or the nuclear envelope.**
Free ribosomes work in the cytosol; bound ribosomes make proteins destined for membranes, organelles, or export.

**24. C — Mitochondrion.**
The endomembrane system comprises the nuclear envelope, ER, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, vacuoles, and plasma membrane — connected either directly or by vesicle transfer. Mitochondria are not part of it.

**25. D — Secretory glycoprotein synthesis on attached ribosomes.**
That is a rough ER function; smooth ER by definition lacks ribosomes. Smooth ER synthesizes lipids, detoxifies drugs and poisons, and stores calcium ions.

**26. B — It produces membrane distributed via transport vesicles.**
Rough ER also has bound ribosomes that make glycoproteins (proteins covalently bonded to carbohydrates) and packages secretory proteins in transport vesicles.

**27. C — Cisternae.**
Cristae are mitochondrial infoldings; thylakoids and grana are chloroplast structures.

**28. B — Cisternae move in a cis-to-trans direction as they mature.**
Vesicles from the ER fuse to form new cis cisternae; some vesicles also transport proteins backward to less mature cisternae or back to the ER.

**29. A — In the acidic environment inside the lysosome.**
Lysosomal enzymes and membranes are made by rough ER, processed in the Golgi, and some lysosomes bud from the trans face of the Golgi.

**30. C — Autophagy.**
Phagocytosis is engulfment of an external particle forming a food vacuole; the lysosome then fuses with and digests the vacuole contents.

**31. B — Pump excess water out of the cell.**
Freshwater protists live in a hypotonic environment and constantly take in water by osmosis.

**32. A — Contains sap and stores inorganic ions.**
The central vacuole also plays a major role in plant cell growth. Vacuoles are large vesicles derived from the ER and Golgi.

**33. D — Direct connection to the nuclear envelope.**
Mitochondria and chloroplasts are **not** part of the endomembrane system and are not continuous with the nuclear envelope. The other three observations are the classic lines of evidence for endosymbiosis.

**34. B — An oxygen-using, nonphotosynthetic bacterial cell.**
That endosymbiont evolved into the mitochondrion. Later, at least one such cell took up a photosynthetic bacterium, which evolved into the chloroplast.

**35. B — Cristae.**
Cristae provide a large surface area for the enzymes that synthesize ATP.

**36. B — Intermembrane space and mitochondrial matrix.**
Some metabolic steps of cellular respiration are catalyzed in the matrix, which also contains ribosomes and DNA.

**37. B — Thylakoids.**
A stack of thylakoids is a granum (plural, grana).

**38. B — Stroma.**
The chloroplast is one member of a group of plant organelles called plastids.

**39. A — Single membrane; forms hydrogen peroxide.**
Peroxisomes remove hydrogen atoms from various substances and transfer them to oxygen. Some break fatty acids into smaller molecules for respiration; in the liver they detoxify alcohol and other harmful compounds. How they relate to other organelles is not known.

**40. B — Convert fatty acids to sugar for the seedling.**
Glyoxysomes are specialized peroxisomes in fat-storing seed tissue.

**41. C — Microtubules; tubulin dimers.**
Microtubules are hollow tubes about 25 nm in diameter with a 15-nm lumen, built from dimers of alpha-tubulin and beta-tubulin. They shape the cell, guide organelle movement, and separate chromosomes during cell division.

**42. A — 7 nm.**
Microfilaments (actin filaments) are the thinnest components — two intertwined strands of actin. Intermediate filaments are 8–12 nm; microtubules are 25 nm.

**43. C — Intermediate filaments.**
Built from proteins such as keratins, they are more permanent than the other two classes, support cell shape, anchor the nucleus and certain organelles, and form the nuclear lamina.

**44. B — Centrosome near the nucleus.**
In animal cells the centrosome contains a pair of centrioles, though other eukaryotic cells organize microtubules without centrosomes containing centrioles.

**45. B — Nine triplets of microtubules in a ring.**
This "9 + 0" triplet arrangement is also seen in the basal body, where the two central microtubules of the cilium terminate.

**46. A — Microtubule pattern in a motile cilium or flagellum.**
Nine outer doublets surround two single central microtubules, held together by flexible cross-linking proteins and radial spokes, all sheathed in an extension of the plasma membrane.

**47. C — Dynein.**
Dynein has two "feet" that walk along the microtubules; because the microtubules are held in place, the walking causes the structure to **bend** rather than slide.

**48. B — Number per cell and beating pattern.**
Motile cilia are numerous and use a rapid power stroke perpendicular to the cilium axis followed by a slower recovery stroke; a flagellum is limited to one or a few per cell and undulates, driving the cell along the flagellum's axis. Both share the same 9 + 2 microtubule structure.

**49. B — Actin–myosin interactions.**
Microfilaments that function in motility contain myosin in addition to actin. Cells crawl by extending pseudopodia; in plant cells a circular flow of cytoplasm is driven by myosin motors moving along actin tracks.

**50. A — Plasmodesmata are functionally similar to gap junctions.**
Both provide cytoplasmic channels between adjacent cells for water, ions, sugars, amino acids, and other small molecules. B and C are reversed: **desmosomes** act like rivets fastening cells into sheets (anchored by keratin intermediate filaments), while **tight junctions** press neighboring membranes together to form a continuous seal against leakage of extracellular fluid. Integrins are receptor proteins **in** the plasma membrane that bind ECM proteins such as fibronectin.

## SECTION D: FIGURE ANSWER KEY

### Figure C3: Animal cell — organelles

**Callouts:** **1** Nucleus · **2** Nucleolus · **3** Rough ER · **4** Smooth ER · **5** Golgi apparatus · **6** Mitochondrion · **7** Lysosome · **8** Plasma membrane · **9** Free ribosomes · **10** Centrosome

**C3.1 B — Structure 2, the nucleolus**
The nucleolus is a dense region within the nucleus where rRNA is transcribed and combined with proteins to form ribosomal subunits. The subunits then leave through the nuclear pores.

**C3.2 A — Structure 3, the rough ER**
Ribosomes on the rough ER synthesise proteins that enter the ER lumen and are destined for secretion, for the plasma membrane, or for other organelles.

**C3.3 B — Structure 4, the smooth ER**
Smooth ER lacks bound ribosomes. Its enzymes synthesise lipids and steroid hormones, store calcium ions, and add hydroxyl groups to drugs to make them more soluble.

**C3.4 A — Structure 5, the Golgi apparatus**
Transport vesicles from the ER fuse with the *cis* face of the Golgi. Products are modified as they progress through the cisternae and depart from the *trans* face.

**C3.5 C — Structure 7, the lysosome**
Lysosomes carry hydrolytic enzymes that work best in an acidic environment. They digest food particles taken in by phagocytosis and recycle the cell's own damaged organelles by autophagy.

**C3.6 C — Structure 6, the mitochondrion**
Cellular respiration takes place in the mitochondrion, whose inner membrane is folded into cristae that greatly increase the surface area available for the enzymes and carriers of the electron transport chain.

**C3.7 B — They are the same ribosomes doing different jobs**
Free and bound ribosomes are structurally identical and interchangeable. What differs is the destination of the polypeptide being made — a signal sequence on the growing chain is what directs a ribosome to the ER.

**C3.8 C — Structure 10, the centrosome containing a pair of centrioles**
Animal cells organise their microtubules from a centrosome that contains centrioles. Plant cells also organise microtubules, but they do so without centrosomes containing centrioles.

### Figure C4: Extracellular matrix of an animal cell

**Callouts:** **1** Collagen · **2** Fibronectin · **3** Proteoglycan complex · **4** Integrin · **5** Plasma membrane · **6** Microfilaments

**C4.1 B — Collagen**
Collagen fibres are embedded in a woven network of proteoglycans. Collagen accounts for about half of the total protein in the human body.

**C4.2 B — It links the matrix to the cytoskeleton**
Integrins are membrane receptor proteins with a binding site for ECM proteins on the extracellular side and, on the cytoplasmic side, an attachment to microfilaments. They can transmit signals in both directions.

**C4.3 A — Fibronectin**
Fibronectins are ECM glycoproteins that bind to integrins, physically connecting the matrix outside to the cytoskeleton inside.

**C4.4 B — A proteoglycan complex**
A proteoglycan molecule is up to 95% carbohydrate by mass. Hundreds of them can attach to a single long polysaccharide, forming the large complex shown.

**C4.5 D — Structure 6, the microfilaments**
Everything above the plasma membrane in this diagram — collagen, fibronectin and the proteoglycan complexes — is extracellular. The microfilaments below it are part of the cytoskeleton.

**C4.6 A — Collagen → fibronectin → integrin → microfilaments**
Because integrins bridge the membrane, a mechanical or chemical change in the matrix can be relayed through the cytoskeleton to the nucleus, altering which genes are expressed.
