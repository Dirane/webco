## SECTION B: ANSWER KEY WITH EXPLANATIONS

**1. A — Amphipathic.**
Phospholipids contain hydrophobic ("water-fearing") tails and a hydrophilic ("water-loving") head. Most membrane proteins are also amphipathic, with hydrophilic regions facing the cytosol and extracellular fluid and hydrophobic regions embedded in the bilayer.

**2. B — Proteins bobbing in a fluid phospholipid bilayer.**
The model accounts both for the fluidity of the lipid and for the "mosaic" of diverse proteins, likened to a tile mosaic.

**3. B — They often form groups that carry out common functions.**
Protein composition also varies among different cells of an organism and among the different internal membranes of a single cell.

**4. C — Weak hydrophobic interactions.**
Because the interactions are weak rather than covalent, most lipids and some proteins can drift laterally within the membrane.

**5. C — Flip-flopping between layers.**
Lateral movement is common and rapid; transverse flip-flop requires the hydrophilic head to cross the hydrophobic core and is therefore rare.

**6. B — Some membrane proteins move laterally.**
The intermixing of mouse and human markers within one hour in the hybrid cell (heterokaryon) is direct evidence of lateral protein mobility.

**7. B — Kinks prevent tight packing.**
Saturated tails are straight and pack closely, increasing viscosity; unsaturated tails are kinked at cis double bonds, creating spaces and enhancing fluidity. The temperature at which a membrane solidifies depends on its lipid types.

**8. B — Restrains phospholipid movement, reducing fluidity.**
Cholesterol has variable, temperature-dependent effects — it is often described as a fluidity "buffer."

**9. A — Hinders solidification by disrupting regular packing.**
Plants use different but related steroid lipids for the same buffering role.

**10. A — High proportion of unsaturated hydrocarbon tails.**
This keeps the membrane fluid at low temperatures — an evolutionary adaptation of membrane lipid composition to environment.

**11. B — Prevent membrane solidification during winter.**
Organisms in variable-temperature environments can adjust lipid composition in response to temperature change.

**12. B — Membranes that are too fluid cannot support protein function.**
Membranes must be fluid to work properly, since fluidity affects both permeability and the movement of transport proteins — but there is an upper limit.

**13. B — Penetrate the hydrophobic core.**
Peripheral proteins are simply bound to the membrane surface and do not enter the bilayer's interior.

**14. B — Nonpolar amino acids, often coiled into alpha helices.**
Nonpolar side chains are compatible with the hydrophobic interior of the bilayer.

**15. B — Transmembrane protein.**
Transmembrane proteins are a subset of integral proteins that span the membrane completely.

**16. C — Storage of hereditary information.**
The six listed functions are transport, enzymatic activity, signal transduction, cell-cell recognition, intercellular joining, and attachment to the cytoskeleton and ECM. Hereditary information is stored in DNA, not membrane proteins.

**17. B — A co-receptor, either CCR5 or CXCR4.**
Which co-receptor is used depends on the strain of HIV.

**18. A — Immune to infection by strains that use CCR5.**
Drugs are being developed to mask CCR5 and block HIV entry in nonimmune individuals infected by CCR5-using strains.

**19. B — Glycolipids; glycoproteins.**
The diversity of these surface carbohydrates lets them serve as markers that distinguish one cell type from another in cell-cell recognition.

**20. B — The extracellular (outer) face.**
Membranes have distinct inside and outside faces. Carbohydrates are added in the ER and modified in the Golgi; when a vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane, the vesicle's inner face becomes the membrane's outer face, positioning the carbohydrates outside the cell.

**21. C — Hydrophobic (nonpolar) molecules.**
They dissolve in the lipid bilayer and cross rapidly. The hydrophobic interior impedes hydrophilic molecules such as sugars, water, and ions, which pass slowly if at all.

**22. A — Provide a hydrophilic tunnel.**
Carrier proteins instead bind the solute and change shape to shuttle it across. Neither necessarily uses ATP — both function in facilitated diffusion.

**23. B — Channel proteins of four polypeptide subunits.**
Each subunit forms a channel for the passage of water, greatly increasing the rate at which water crosses the membrane.

**24. C — 3 billion.**
Up to about 3 billion water molecules per second can pass through.

**25. B — Specific for the substances they move.**
The selective permeability of a membrane depends on both the lipid bilayer and the particular transport proteins it contains.

**26. C — As many molecules cross in one direction as in the other.**
Dynamic equilibrium means molecular motion continues, but there is no **net** movement.

**27. A — Each diffuses down its own concentration gradient.**
Each substance moves independently, unaffected by the concentrations of other substances — so net diffusion of one dye can occur toward the side that has the greater **total** solute concentration.

**28. B — No energy is expended by the cell.**
The concentration gradient itself represents potential energy that drives diffusion, although the rate also depends on membrane permeability to that substance.

**29. B — Free water across a selectively permeable membrane.**
"Free water" means water molecules not clustered around solute particles.

**30. B — Lower solute concentration to higher solute concentration.**
The side with less solute has more free water molecules available to cross. Water keeps moving until solute concentrations are equal on both sides.

**31. B — Solutes that cannot cross the membrane, relative to inside the cell.**
Tonicity is the ability of a surrounding solution to cause a cell to gain or lose water.

**32. B — Lose water, shrivel, and may die.**
In a hypertonic solution, external solute concentration exceeds internal, so net water diffusion is outward. Cells without walls cannot tolerate the loss.

**33. B — Gain water, swell, and may lyse.**
Net water diffusion is inward, and a wall-less cell may burst.

**34. C — Isotonic.**
Water crosses at equal rates in both directions, so cell volume is stable. Organisms in hypotonic or hypertonic environments need osmoregulation.

**35. B — Contractile vacuole pumps excess water out.**
This is a classic osmoregulatory adaptation. Bacteria and archaea in hypersaline environments have the opposite problem and possess mechanisms to keep water from leaving.

**36. B — Turgor pressure.**
At this point the cell is turgid (very firm), which is the healthy state for most plant cells — the reverse of the animal-cell situation.

**37. B — Flaccid, and the plant wilts.**
With no net water uptake, the wall exerts no back-pressure.

**38. B — The plasma membrane pulls away from the cell wall.**
The membrane detaches at multiple locations; the plant wilts and may die.

**39. A — Transport proteins speed passive movement down gradients.**
Both channel proteins and carrier proteins carry out facilitated diffusion; no energy is required because the solute moves down its gradient.

**40. B — Open or close in response to a stimulus.**
For example, potassium ion channels in nerve cells open in response to an electrical stimulus; other gated channels respond to the binding of a specific chemical.

**41. B — Down their gradients, with no energy input, via a shape change.**
The subtle shape change moves the solute-binding site across the membrane and can be triggered by binding and release of the transported molecule.

**42. A — Uses energy to move solutes against their gradients.**
This lets cells maintain internal solute concentrations that differ sharply from the surrounding environment.

**43. B — They are all carrier proteins.**
Channel proteins cannot pump; only carrier proteins undergo the conformational cycle required to move a solute uphill.

**44. A — Higher K⁺ inside, lower Na⁺ inside.**
These gradients are established and maintained by the sodium-potassium pump.

**45. B — 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in.**
Because more positive charge leaves than enters, the pump is electrogenic and contributes to the membrane potential.

**46. B — Transfer of a phosphate group from ATP.**
Binding of three cytoplasmic Na⁺ stimulates phosphorylation; phosphorylation changes the protein's shape and lowers its affinity for Na⁺, which is released outside. The new shape has high affinity for K⁺; K⁺ binding triggers loss of the phosphate, restoring the original shape and releasing K⁺ inside.

**47. B — Electrochemical gradient.**
It combines a chemical force (the concentration gradient) and an electrical force (the effect of membrane potential). An ion diffuses down its electrochemical gradient. The inside of the cell is negative relative to the outside, favoring cation entry and anion exit.

**48. B — Proton pump.**
It actively transports H⁺ out of the cell. In animals the main electrogenic pump is the sodium-potassium pump. An electrogenic pump generates voltage across a membrane, storing energy for cellular work.

**49. B — Downhill diffusion of H⁺ through a sucrose-H⁺ cotransporter.**
Proton pumps first build the H⁺ gradient using ATP; the cotransporter then couples H⁺ re-entry to sucrose uptake. This is how plants load sucrose into their veins. Animal cells use an analogous Na⁺-glucose cotransporter in the intestinal lining — the basis of oral rehydration therapy, in which a concentrated salt-and-glucose solution restores sodium lost during diarrhea.

**50. A — Cholesterol accumulates because LDL uptake is impaired.**
Human cells normally take in cholesterol, carried in low-density lipoproteins, by receptor-mediated endocytosis. Without functional receptors, LDL is not internalized; lipids build up in blood vessels, narrowing them and risking heart damage or stroke.

## SECTION D: FIGURE ANSWER KEY

### Figure C5: Fluid mosaic model of the plasma membrane

**Callouts:** **1** Hydrophilic head · **2** Hydrophobic tail · **3** Cholesterol · **4** Integral protein · **5** Glycoprotein · **6** Glycolipid · **7** Peripheral protein · **8** Microfilaments · **9** Fibres of the ECM

**C5.1 A — Structure 1, the hydrophilic head**
A phospholipid is amphipathic. Its phosphate-containing head is hydrophilic and faces water; its two fatty acid tails are hydrophobic and are turned inward, away from water.

**C5.2 B — Hydrophobic regions of nonpolar amino acids**
The hydrophobic regions of an integral protein consist of stretches of nonpolar amino acids, typically coiled into α helices, which sit comfortably in the hydrophobic interior of the bilayer.

**C5.3 B — Less fluid**
Cholesterol acts as a fluidity buffer. At warm temperatures it restrains phospholipid movement and reduces fluidity; at cool temperatures it hinders tight packing and so prevents the membrane from solidifying.

**C5.4 B — It is bound to the surface, not embedded**
Peripheral proteins are not embedded in the lipid bilayer. They are appendages loosely bound to the membrane surface, often to the exposed parts of integral proteins.

**C5.5 B — Cell–cell recognition**
The carbohydrates of glycolipids and glycoproteins vary between species, between individuals and between cell types, which lets cells identify one another — the basis of the ABO blood groups and of an immune system's recognition of foreign cells.

**C5.6 C — Structure 8, the microfilaments**
Carbohydrate-bearing glycoproteins and glycolipids and the ECM fibres are all on the extracellular face. Cytoskeletal filaments attach on the cytoplasmic face and help maintain cell shape.

**C5.7 B — The assortment of proteins in the bilayer**
"Fluid" describes the drifting of the molecules within the plane of the membrane; "mosaic" describes the collage of different proteins studded in and attached to it.

**C5.8 A — More fluid**
Kinks at the double bonds of unsaturated hydrocarbon tails keep the phospholipids from packing closely together, so the membrane stays fluid to a lower temperature.

### Figure C6: Routes across the plasma membrane

**Callouts:** **1** Simple diffusion through the bilayer · **2** Channel protein · **3** Carrier protein · **4** ATP-powered pump · **5** ATP · **6** Concentration gradient

**C6.1 D — Route 4**
Routes 1, 2 and 3 all move solute down its concentration gradient and require no energy from the cell. Route 4 moves solute up its gradient and must be powered.

**C6.2 B — It moves solute against the gradient**
Active transport moves a solute from where it is less concentrated to where it is more concentrated. Because this works against diffusion, it requires an input of energy.

**C6.3 B — Small nonpolar molecules**
Hydrophobic molecules dissolve in the lipid bilayer and cross it easily. Ions and large polar molecules cannot pass through the hydrophobic core unaided.

**C6.4 A — 2 is an open passage; 3 binds and changes shape**
Channel proteins form a hydrophilic tunnel that particular molecules or ions can slip through. Carrier proteins instead bind the solute and undergo a shape change that shuttles it across. Both are facilitated diffusion — both are passive.

**C6.5 B — By phosphorylating the transport protein**
ATP powers active transport by transferring its terminal phosphate group to the transport protein. The resulting shape change moves the solute across the membrane.

**C6.6 B — Structure 2, a channel protein**
An aquaporin is a channel protein that provides a passage for water molecules. It does not change the direction of osmosis — water still moves down its own concentration gradient — it only speeds the process up.

**C6.7 C — Routes 1, 2 and 3**
Passive transport is driven by the solute's own concentration gradient — it is spontaneous and needs no cellular energy. Only the pump at 4 would stop.
