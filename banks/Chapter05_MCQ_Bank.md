## SECTION B: ANSWER KEY WITH EXPLANATIONS

**1. C — Lipids.**
Carbohydrates, proteins, and nucleic acids are polymers built from monomers. Lipids are a diverse group of hydrophobic molecules that are neither true polymers nor, strictly speaking, macromolecules.

**2. B — Monomers.**
A polymer is a long molecule of many similar building blocks; those repeating building blocks are monomers. "Macromolecule" refers to the large polymer itself.

**3. B — Removal of a water molecule as a covalent bond forms.**
In dehydration synthesis, an –OH from one monomer and an –H from the other are released as H₂O, and a covalent bond joins the two monomers.

**4. C — Shorter chains, with –H on one fragment and –OH on the other.**
Hydrolysis is essentially the reverse of dehydration: water is added, the bond breaks, and the H and OH from water cap the two new ends.

**5. B — Enzymes.**
Enzymes are specialized macromolecules that speed up chemical reactions, including both the synthesis and the breakdown of polymers.

**6. C — A huge variety of polymers can be built from a small set of monomers.**
A cell has thousands of different macromolecules. They vary among cells of one organism, vary more within a species, and vary most between species — yet all are assembled from a limited monomer set.

**7. A — CH₂O.**
Monosaccharide formulas are usually multiples of CH₂O; glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) is the most common monosaccharide.

**8. B — An aldose.**
When the carbonyl is terminal it is an aldehyde, so the sugar is an aldose. A ketose has an internal carbonyl (a ketone).

**9. C — Fructose.**
Fructose, dihydroxyacetone, and ribulose are ketoses. Glucose, galactose, glyceraldehyde, and ribose are aldoses.

**10. B — Pentose.**
A pentose has five carbons and the formula C₅H₁₀O₅. Ribose is a component of RNA; ribulose is a photosynthesis intermediate.

**11. C — The spatial placement of a hydroxyl group on one asymmetric carbon.**
Glucose and galactose are both hexoses and aldoses; they differ only in whether the –OH on carbon 4 lies to the right (glucose) or left (galactose).

**12. C — Form ring structures, which are strongly favored at equilibrium.**
Although often drawn as linear skeletons, in aqueous solution glucose closes into a ring when carbon 1 bonds to the oxygen attached to carbon 5; equilibrium greatly favors the ring.

**13. D — Glycosidic linkage.**
A glycosidic linkage is the covalent bond between two monosaccharides. Peptide bonds join amino acids; ester linkages join fatty acids to glycerol; phosphodiester linkages join nucleotides.

**14. A — Two glucose monomers joined by a 1–4 glycosidic linkage.**
In maltose, carbon 1 of one glucose is joined through oxygen to carbon 4 of the second glucose.

**15. B — Glucose joined to fructose by a 1–2 linkage.**
Sucrose is a glucose–fructose disaccharide; the fructose forms a five-membered ring even though it is a hexose.

**16. B — As granules within chloroplasts and other plastids.**
Starch is the storage polysaccharide of plants, deposited as granules in plastids.

**17. C — Amylose.**
Amylose is the simplest starch: an unbranched chain of glucose with 1–4 linkages. Amylopectin is branched, with 1–6 linkages at branch points.

**18. B — Stored mainly in liver and muscle cells and more branched than amylopectin.**
Glycogen is the animal storage polysaccharide; its hydrolysis releases glucose when sugar demand increases.

**19. B — Built from beta glucose monomers, with every other monomer inverted.**
Glucose rings exist in alpha (–OH on C1 down) and beta (–OH up) forms. Starch is a 1–4 polymer of alpha glucose and is helical; cellulose is a 1–4 polymer of beta glucose, straight and rigid.

**20. C — Hydrogen bonding between hydroxyl groups of adjacent molecules.**
Because cellulose chains are straight and unbranched, hydroxyls on parallel molecules hydrogen-bond, producing the microfibrils of the plant cell wall.

**21. B — Human enzymes hydrolyze alpha linkages but not beta linkages.**
Enzymes that digest starch cannot attack the beta linkages of cellulose, so cellulose passes through the digestive tract as insoluble fiber.

**22. C — Symbiotic relationships with cellulose-digesting microbes.**
Many herbivores, from cows to termites, house microbes whose enzymes hydrolyze cellulose.

**23. B — Arthropod exoskeletons and fungal cell walls.**
Chitin's monomer is N-acetylglucosamine, a glucose derivative in which the alcohol on carbon 2 is replaced by an acetamido group.

**24. C — They mix poorly, if at all, with water.**
Lipids consist mostly of hydrocarbon regions; hydrophobicity, not a shared monomer, is the unifying feature.

**25. A — Glycerol and three fatty acids joined by ester linkages.**
Glycerol is a three-carbon alcohol with a hydroxyl on each carbon; each fatty acid is joined by a dehydration reaction forming an ester linkage.

**26. B — Maximum hydrogens and no double bonds.**
Saturated fats are typically solid at room temperature and are common in animal tissue.

**27. B — Kinks caused by double bonds prevent close packing.**
Cis double bonds bend the hydrocarbon tails so the molecules cannot pack tightly enough to solidify.

**28. B — Converts unsaturated to saturated fats and can create trans double bonds.**
Trans fats produced by hydrogenation may contribute even more than saturated fats to cardiovascular disease.

**29. B — Adipose cells.**
The major function of fats is energy storage; adipose tissue additionally cushions vital organs and insulates the body.

**30. B — Two fatty acids and a phosphate group attached to glycerol.**
This makes the molecule amphipathic: hydrophobic tails plus a hydrophilic phosphate head.

**31. B — Hydrophilic phosphate heads, hydrophobic tails.**
Self-assembly places heads in contact with water on both surfaces and tails toward the interior — the basis of the membrane bilayer.

**32. A — Four fused rings.**
Attached chemical groups distinguish one steroid from another.

**33. B — Component of animal cell membranes and precursor of other steroids.**
High blood cholesterol may contribute to cardiovascular disease.

**34. C — More than 50%.**
Proteins are the most abundant class of macromolecule by dry mass in most cells.

**35. D — Ovalbumin is a *storage* protein, not a motor protein.**
Ovalbumin (egg white) supplies amino acids to the developing embryo. Motor/contractile proteins include actin and myosin.

**36. B — 20.**
All proteins are built from the same set of 20 amino acids.

**37. C — Side chains (R groups).**
The amino group, carboxyl group, alpha carbon, and hydrogen are common to all amino acids; only the R group differs, giving each its chemical properties (nonpolar, polar, acidic, or basic).

**38. B — Peptide bond.**
A dehydration reaction joins the carboxyl group of one amino acid to the amino group of the next.

**39. B — To the carboxyl end (C-terminus).**
The chain grows from the N-terminus toward the C-terminus, one amino acid at a time.

**40. A — Unique sequence of amino acids, genetically determined.**
Primary structure is like the order of letters in a long word and is inherited.

**41. B — Hydrogen bonds between backbone constituents.**
In the alpha helix, hydrogen bonding occurs between every fourth amino acid; in beta pleated sheets, hydrogen bonds link parallel beta strands. Note that these bonds involve the backbone, not the R groups.

**42. C — Disulfide bridges between cysteine side chains.**
Two cysteine sulfhydryl (–SH) groups brought together by folding form a strong covalent –S–S– bridge. Hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and van der Waals interactions are all weak (noncovalent).

**43. D — Quaternary structure.**
Quaternary structure arises when two or more polypeptide subunits associate into one functional macromolecule. Collagen (three polypeptides coiled like a rope) is another example.

**44. B — A single amino acid substitution in hemoglobin.**
The substitution of valine changes the beta subunit's surface so that hemoglobin molecules associate into fibers via hydrophobic interactions, deforming red blood cells into a sickle shape and reducing oxygen-carrying capacity. It illustrates how primary structure dictates function.

**45. B — Denaturation.**
A denatured protein is biologically inactive. Denaturation is sometimes reversible when the denaturing agent is removed — but not always. Very high fevers can be fatal because blood proteins denature.

**46. B — Cryo-electron microscopy.**
Cryo-EM freezes specimens rapidly and does not require crystallization, complementing X-ray crystallography. Bioinformatics predicts structure computationally from sequence.

**47. B — A nitrogenous base, a pentose sugar, and one or more phosphate groups.**
The portion without the phosphate (base + sugar) is a nucleoside.

**48. B — Cytosine, thymine, and uracil.**
Pyrimidines have a single six-membered ring. Purines (adenine and guanine) have a six-membered ring fused to a five-membered ring.

**49. B — Antiparallel.**
The two polynucleotides spiral around an imaginary axis with backbones running in opposite directions. "Complementary" refers to the specific A–T and G–C base pairing, a different concept.

**50. C — In RNA, uracil replaces thymine and pairs with adenine.**
DNA uses deoxyribose and is a double helix; RNA uses ribose and is single-stranded but variable in form, and complementary pairing can occur between two RNA molecules or within parts of the same molecule.

## SECTION D: FIGURE ANSWER KEY

### Figure C1: Amino acid — general structure

**Callouts:** **1** Amino group · **2** Carboxyl group · **3** Alpha (α) carbon · **4** Side chain (R group) · **5** Hydrogen atom

**C1.1 B — The amino group**
The –NH₂ group gives the amino acid the first half of its name. It acts as a base, accepting a hydrogen ion from the surrounding solution.

**C1.2 D — Structure 4, the side chain**
Structures 1, 2, 3 and 5 are identical in every amino acid. Only the R group varies, and it is the R group that makes an amino acid nonpolar, polar, acidic or basic.

**C1.3 C — The carboxyl group of one and the amino group of the next**
The carboxyl group (2) of one amino acid is positioned next to the amino group (1) of the next; a water molecule is removed and the resulting covalent bond is a peptide bond.

**C1.4 C — Four**
The α carbon is bonded to the amino group, the carboxyl group, a hydrogen atom and the R group — four different partners, which is what makes it asymmetric.

**C1.5 B — 1 becomes –NH₃⁺ and 2 becomes –COO⁻**
The carboxyl group is acidic and gives up a proton; the amino group is basic and accepts one. At cellular pH the amino acid therefore carries both a positive and a negative charge, although the diagram shows the uncharged form for clarity.

**C1.6 B — Nonpolar and hydrophobic**
Side chains built only from carbon and hydrogen share electrons evenly, so they are nonpolar and do not interact favourably with water. These residues tend to be buried in a protein's interior.

### Figure C2: Polynucleotide — two linked nucleotides

**Callouts:** **1** Phosphate group · **2** Pentose sugar · **3** Nitrogenous base · **4** Phosphodiester linkage · **5** 5′ end (free phosphate) · **6** 3′ end (free —OH)

**C2.1 B — The sugar–phosphate backbone**
Alternating sugars and phosphate groups form the covalent backbone of a polynucleotide. The bases are appendages that project from this backbone.

**C2.2 B — Phosphate + sugar + base**
A sugar joined to a nitrogenous base is a nucleoside; adding one or more phosphate groups makes it a nucleotide.

**C2.3 B — The 3′ carbon of one sugar to the 5′ phosphate of the next**
This covalent phosphodiester linkage gives the backbone a repeating sugar–phosphate–sugar pattern and gives the strand its directionality.

**C2.4 B — Deoxyribose**
DNA's pentose lacks the hydroxyl group on the 2′ carbon that ribose carries — hence *deoxy*ribose. RNA uses ribose.

**C2.5 B — End 6 — the 3′ end**
A polynucleotide has a free phosphate at the 5′ end and a free hydroxyl at the 3′ end. Nucleotides can only be added to the free 3′ hydroxyl, so strands elongate 5′ → 3′.

**C2.6 B — Thymine**
Adenine pairs with thymine (two hydrogen bonds) and guanine pairs with cytosine (three). In RNA, adenine pairs with uracil instead.
